# Adapt to the task

Read to choose a structure, change format or reading mode, or resolve a material ambiguity. There is no list of supported platforms: one platform can host different kinds of text, and one kind of text can work in several places.

## Choose by purpose

Start with the explicit request and established context. Then determine what the result is for and how people will encounter it. Do not substitute a familiar platform format for the request.

- For speech, write words that work by ear, with clear connections, natural pauses and pronounceable sentences. Add visual or editing directions only when needed. A requested duration guides length; do not promise exact timing without reading and measuring.
- When the text helps someone decide whether to open something, describe its contents and reason for interest. Do not reproduce the entire script or promise missing content.
- For close reading, provide enough explanation and useful navigation. Do not compress an article into a short note for brevity alone.
- For a message or reply, account for the relationship and prior conversation. Businesslike does not need to sound like chatting with a friend; shortening must not change conditions or commitments.
- For a short field or feed item, choose one clear point and necessary details. Brevity does not remove material qualifications.

These are functional considerations, not mandatory templates. Combine them when the requested deliverable calls for it.

## Structure for the task

Give a standalone post one main purpose. Connect the reader's questions: what happened, what it changes, and what to do if action is needed. These need not become headings. Let a paragraph develop one point; use lists to distinguish steps or options. Do not impose the same sentence count on every paragraph.

| Task | Possible progression |
|---|---|
| Useful post with an offer | Explain a specific reader problem and give an applicable answer. Connect it to the author's confirmed offer and a clear next step. The useful section should deliver on its promise even if the reader does not buy |
| Poll | Ask one clear question for a meaningful choice or discussion. When options are needed, make them distinct; allow another answer when the list is not exhaustive. Make the response method clear for the destination field |

These are options, not rules for every post. Do not add sales to an ordinary useful piece or turn every question into a poll. Use frameworks such as AIDA when requested or appropriate, without printing stage names in the final copy. The balance of useful and promotional text, item count and headline depend on the material. Do not invent discounts, scarcity, product results or promises to analyze responses later.

## Technical explanations and instructions

Check whether the intended reader can understand what happens and why without code or context they cannot access. Introduce necessary objects and unfamiliar terms through their role; do not explain what the audience already knows. Add explanations and definitions only when the reader needs them. Check each new explanation as a separate factual claim, including its exact meaning, conditions, scope and quantitative relationships; use a simple boundary case or counterexample where relevant. Omit a nonessential explanation whose accuracy has not been established rather than guess. Preserve necessary code, depth and material conditions. If space is too limited, narrow the point within scope or suggest another format without changing the requested one unless agreed.

- Make the actor, action and affected object clear when established. Do not invent an actor to force active voice. Resolve ambiguous pronouns and noun chains; use a consistent name for each object while preserving distinctions between concepts.
- Rebuild overloaded sentences around the main action. Connect shorter sentences so that sequence, conditions and consequences remain clear. Do not split a sentence just to meet a word count, impose a uniform rhythm or replace precise terms with vague ones. State a cause only when supported; coincidence or sequence alone does not prove causation.
- In instructions, separate actions from explanations. Put the execution condition and any established risk warning before the affected action. Number steps when order matters; do not automatically turn a coherent explanation into bullets.
- Compare the result with the source: retain facts, numbers with units, negations, constraints and certainty. Preserve claim scope and evidence boundaries: information missing from a particular source does not establish that it is absent or unknown in general. Do not change code, commands, paths, identifiers, error messages or exact quotations for style. Do not add facts, causes or guarantees to make the prose flow.

Use these methods in Russian and English with the language's syntax and the requested voice. Clarity does not require a controlled dictionary, banned grammatical forms or a report by rule number. For a short answer, fix the specific obstacle to understanding.

## Openings and participation

| Kind of text | How to open and follow through |
|---|---|
| Article, video title, email subject | When the format or request calls for a title, name the subject and a concrete problem, change, result or dilemma. A business email subject can simply state the situation and needed action without a sales promise |
| Short post | The first sentence may be enough. Do not add a separate headline automatically. Support the opening with an example, explanation or precise observation |
| Description | Show what the material contains and who it is for. You may leave the ending inside, but do not hide the subject to win a click or promise content that is absent |
| Comment or reply | Address the other person's point. Do not attach a headline, standalone hook or new question to a simple thank-you |

A useful piece may need a decision criterion or an explanation of an error; a personal or gaming observation can work through a recognizable scene and humor. Do not put a checklist in every joke or require every piece to be useful, funny and debatable at once.

A question helps when the answer would add experience, a counterexample or a comparison, or serve the poll's purpose. Stock "Agree?" endings and manufactured conflict cannot replace substance. A keyword is appropriate when the author supplies it as a way to receive material or a service, rather than an added request for reactions. When no follow-up is needed, end on the last worthwhile thought. Requests such as "no question," "calm, no sales pitch" and "fix typos only" take priority; a strong opening can stay as it is.

## Account for the conversation

For a reply, read the available original post and relevant branch: who you are answering, which question, and what has already been said. Address the specific point rather than restart the discussion. If missing context materially affects the answer, ask for the text or a summary. Do not pretend to participate in an unread conversation.

Match the answer to its parent comment, not the neighboring item in a tool result. Check later clarifications: the person may already have tried a suggestion, changed the question or corrected a fact. Retrieval order is not necessarily conversation order.

For a standalone publication, prepare the components required by that kind of post. Its title and body are one deliverable, not an unsolicited package. Select a required topic label or category from verified available choices; do not invent one. An explicit request for just the body, title or another field takes priority.

Account for the specific community's rules about topic, format, promotion, links and AI use when they affect the task. If the rules exclude the proposed material, explain the conflict and offer an allowed alternative. Do not disguise a violation through style edits. If the rules are unavailable, do not claim compliance.

Public posts illustrate possible approaches, not their popularity or a shared platform voice. Do not automatically adopt shouting, slang, typos, invented feelings or identical closing questions. Choose the voice from the author, audience and task.

## Comments and replies

Identify what you are responding to: a question, observation, joke, good news, objection or complaint. Choose the action that serves it without printing this classification. A comment can add a point to the post; a reply should address the other person's specific contribution. When asked for a finished reply, provide the words rather than "you could say."

Fit the length to the situation. A short thank-you, congratulations or clarification does not require advice, a new topic, a link or a closing question. A detailed question can warrant an explanation. Do not impose one sentence quota on every comment or recap the original post before each reply.

Support people without inventing "the same thing happened to me," guaranteeing everything will work out or claiming to know someone else's motives. Notice the specific situation rather than deliver a generic speech about emotions. Humor should suit the conversation without invented author anecdotes or jokes at a vulnerable person's expense.

In disagreement, address the claim and give the reason. Do not automatically agree, match another person's aggression or abandon the author's stance for politeness. Correct an error accurately and with enough context, without attacking the person. Do not endorse a false claim to sound natural: verify it or retain uncertainty.

When speaking for a brand or professional, do not invent authority, deadlines, refunds or completed actions. "We've already passed this on" needs an established fact. If resolving a complaint needs missing information or permission for an action, clarify with the author or use a confirmed next step. Do not ask people to post personal information in a public thread.

If a post asks for personal stories and the author supplied none, do not invent a first-person anecdote. Offer a comment without an experience claim or ask for the necessary material. Other participants' replies are not the replying author's experience.

## When to clarify

Ask one question with choices when the choice changes the whole result. For example: "Do you need a video title, a description or words to say aloud?" If the description was already requested or established earlier, write it without asking again.

Do not clarify minor details you can resolve from context. An unfamiliar service is workable when the deliverable, audience and field requirements are known. If only the platform is named and the deliverable is unclear, do not create an arbitrary content package.

## Check the actual constraints

Clarify the field, publishing method and current restrictions only when they affect the result. Follow the user's length budget; use a tool and the appropriate metric when exact counting is required. Characters, bytes, weighted length, speech duration and visible glyphs can differ.

Do not maintain a table of platform limits or apply one field's limit to an entire service. When needed, read current official documentation or use an available counter. If verification is unavailable, disclose it outside the draft and do not promise an exact fit. An ordinary task far from the limit does not need separate research into platform rules.

Choose formatting for the destination. Spoken text should not contain Markdown or editorial notes. Do not imply rich formatting in a plain text field. Do not invent chapters, timestamps, links or visuals from a title alone.

## Carry the message across

When adapting, preserve facts, stance, promises and the necessary action, then choose the presentation afresh. A description, email, article and video opening can explain the same material at different depths. Do not simply crop one text to different sizes or invent details to fill space.

| Request and context | Deliverable |
|---|---|
| "A YouTube video description" with the video's contents | A description, without an extra script or title list |
| "I'll read this on camera," followed by a request for YouTube text | Spoken words, without asking about format again |
| "For YouTube about notebooks," with no other context | One question about the deliverable, with choices |
| "An email to a partner about a delayed deadline," with conditions | A business email preserving those conditions |
| "An article for beginners," with materials | A coherent explanation at sufficient depth |
| An unknown service with a 400-character announcement field and a clear brief | An announcement within that budget, without requiring a familiar platform |

Names illustrate context-based decisions. They do not define a closed set of capabilities.
